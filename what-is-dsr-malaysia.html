What Is DSR in Malaysia and Why Banks Use It

Last updated: May 2026  |  Reading time: ~7 min

Quick Answer DSR (Debt Service Ratio) in Malaysia is the percentage of a borrower's gross monthly income that goes toward debt repayments. Banks use DSR to assess whether a borrower can afford a new loan without being over-indebted. Most Malaysian banks require a post-loan DSR below 60–70%. A high DSR is one of the most common reasons for loan rejection.
This guide is for general information only. It is not financial advice and is not affiliated with any bank or government agency. DSR thresholds vary by bank and borrower profile.

What Is DSR?

Debt Service Ratio (DSR) — sometimes called Debt-to-Income Ratio — measures how much of your gross monthly income is already committed to debt repayments. It is expressed as a percentage.

Banks in Malaysia use DSR as a core eligibility filter for loan applications. If adding a new loan repayment pushes your DSR beyond the bank's acceptable threshold, the application will typically be declined regardless of your income level.

How to Calculate Your DSR

The DSR formula is straightforward:

DSR = (Total Monthly Debt Repayments ÷ Gross Monthly Income) × 100

What counts as "debt repayments":

  • Home loan instalments
  • Car loan monthly payments
  • Personal loan repayments
  • Minimum monthly credit card payments (usually 5% of outstanding balance or the minimum stated on your statement)
  • Study loan repayments (PTPTN)
  • Other financing commitments
Worked Example Gross monthly salary: RM 7,500
Car loan: RM 800/month
PTPTN: RM 300/month
Credit card minimum: RM 250/month
Current DSR: (800 + 300 + 250) ÷ 7,500 × 100 = 18.7%

Adding a home loan repayment of RM 1,800/month:
New DSR = (800 + 300 + 250 + 1,800) ÷ 7,500 × 100 = 42.7% — generally acceptable.

But if the home loan repayment were RM 3,200/month:
New DSR = (800 + 300 + 250 + 3,200) ÷ 7,500 × 100 = 60% — borderline and may be declined depending on the bank.

What DSR Threshold Do Malaysian Banks Use?

There is no single national DSR limit set in law, but Bank Negara Malaysia's responsible lending guidelines influence what banks consider acceptable. In practice:

Income LevelTypical DSR Ceiling
Below RM 3,000/month60%
RM 3,000 – RM 5,000/month60–65%
Above RM 5,000/monthUp to 70% (some banks)
Government employeesSometimes up to 75% (select banks)

* These are approximate industry norms. Each bank sets its own internal DSR policy.

Why Banks Care About DSR

From the bank's perspective, lending to someone with a very high DSR is risky. If most of a borrower's income is already committed to existing debts, there is little buffer to handle unexpected expenses — job loss, medical costs, or even a slight increase in loan repayments due to a rising interest rate.

Bank Negara Malaysia has encouraged banks to practise responsible lending, which includes ensuring borrowers are not over-indebted. This protects both the borrower and the financial system from systemic risk.

What Income Banks Count in the DSR Calculation

Not all income is treated equally. Banks typically consider:

  • Basic salary — fully counted
  • Fixed allowances — counted if consistently paid and reflected in payslips
  • Overtime and variable bonuses — may be partially counted or excluded (typically 50% of average)
  • Rental income — may be counted at 70–80% of the declared amount
  • Freelance or commission income — averaged over 24 months with documentary evidence

Understanding how the bank counts your income can help explain why the maximum loan offered may be lower than what you expected from a simple calculation.

How to Reduce Your DSR Before Applying

Pay Down Credit Card Balances

Even though you might pay your full credit card balance monthly, banks count a minimum monthly payment (typically 5% of the outstanding balance) as a debt commitment in the DSR. Reducing your balance — or cancelling unused cards — lowers the figure counted against you.

Settle or Reduce Smaller Loans

Paying off a personal loan or hire purchase loan with a small remaining balance can free up DSR headroom for a larger new loan.

Extend Your Tenure

A longer loan tenure means a lower monthly repayment, which means a lower DSR for the same loan amount. The trade-off is more total interest paid over the loan period.

Apply With a Co-Borrower

Adding a co-borrower (such as a spouse) combines both incomes, which can lower the effective DSR significantly.

Frequently Asked Questions

What does a 70% DSR mean?

A 70% DSR means 70% of your gross monthly income is committed to debt repayments. This is at or near the upper limit of what most Malaysian banks will accept. At this level, loan approval may be conditional or the loan amount offered may be reduced.

Does PTPTN count toward my DSR?

Yes. PTPTN repayments are included in your total monthly debt obligations when banks calculate your DSR. If you are actively repaying PTPTN, it is counted alongside other loan commitments.

Is DSR calculated on gross or net income?

In Malaysia, DSR is typically calculated using gross monthly income (before EPF, SOCSO, and income tax deductions). However, some banks may use different definitions. Confirm with your bank which income figure they use.

Can I have a co-borrower to reduce my DSR?

Yes. Adding a co-borrower combines incomes for the DSR calculation, which can bring the ratio down into an acceptable range. Both borrowers' credit records and existing debts will be assessed together.

What is the difference between DSR and LTV?

DSR (Debt Service Ratio) measures income versus debt repayments — it is about affordability. LTV (Loan-to-Value) measures the loan amount versus the property value — it is about collateral and down payment. Both are used during home loan assessment but measure different things.
